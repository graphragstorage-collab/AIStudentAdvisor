**Mathematical Induction and Structural Induction in Computer Science (CS101, Department of Computer Science, University of Example)**

This document provides an in-depth exploration of mathematical induction and structural induction, as covered in Week 7 of the CS101 course. Key topics include a review of mathematical induction, strong induction, and recursive definitions, with specific references to sections KR 5.2 and KR 5.3. The document outlines various examples and exercises designed to illustrate the principles of induction, including proofs related to Fibonacci numbers, postage problems, and the Game of Nim. It emphasizes the importance of these concepts in computer science, particularly in algorithm design and analysis. The content is structured to facilitate understanding of both the theoretical and practical applications of induction methods in mathematical reasoning and programming.

Recursively Defined Functions
Definition:  A recursive definition of a function on N consists of two parts.
1. BASIS STEP: Specify the value of the function at 𝑛 = 0 (i.e. f(0)).
2. RECURSIVE STEP: A rule for finding the value of 𝑓(𝑛) from 𝑓(𝑘) for 𝑘 < 𝑛.
A function f(n) is the same as a sequence a0, a1, … , ai  …  where f(i) = ai.
(See recurrence relations as seen in Section 2.4.)

Strong Induction
Strong Induction: To prove that P(n) is true for all positive
integers n, complete two steps:
1. BASIS STEP: Verify that P(1) is true.
2. INDUCTIVE STEP: Show that
[P(1) ∧ P(2) ∧∙∙∙ ∧ P(k)] → P(k + 1)  is true for all positive
integers k.
• Strong Induction are used when we cannot easily prove a
result using mathematical induction.
• Strong induction and the principles of mathematical
induction are equivalent.

Game of Nim: 2-player strategy game
Induction Step: show that P(n) holds.
Let S1 and S2 be the two stacks. W.l.o.g., let S1 be the stack player A
removes coins from.
• Assume player A removes x coins.
• This leaves n-x coins in stack S1 and n coins in S2.
• B plays next and removes x coins from stack S2.
• If n-x is 0, then B wins. Otherwise, 1 ≤ n-x .
• This leaves two stacks of n-x coins each and it is A’s turn.
• By the I.H., Player B can win the game as there are two stacks,
each containing n-x coins,  1 ≤ n-x < n, and it is A’s turn.
Using this strategy, player B will win the game.
